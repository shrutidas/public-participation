Second Iteration · Research Memo

Causal Chains for Public Participation in Science and Technology Policy

Three cases mapped link by link, with evidence and participation instances at every link. Companion to the interactive tool; see the Causal Chain view.
The first memo asked whether public participation would have reduced harm in these cases. This one asks a narrower and more answerable question: at which specific causal link would participation have had to act, and is there evidence that anything did? Mapping the chains produced four corrections to the first iteration's framing and one finding that runs against the project's thesis. Both are below, before the good news.

Section 1What the evidence changed

Five claims from the first iteration did not survive contact with the literature. Each is more interesting corrected than it was as originally stated.

1. PFAS was not a detection failure. It was a disclosure failure.

The first memo framed Stage 1 as "corporations were hoarding information that public and government did not know," with citizen sensing as the natural fix. But EPA's own Office of Research and Development detected the novel compounds in Cape Fear samples collected in summer 2012 (Strynar et al. 2015), and Detlef Knappe emailed the quantifying paper to NC DEQ on 23 November 2016. The public found out on 7 June 2017, from a newspaper. Roughly five years from federal detection, seven months from a peer-reviewed paper landing in a regulator's inbox.

This matters because it changes the prescription. Participation instruments that improve sensing (bucket brigades, community air monitors, citizen science) would not have helped here. Instruments that force notification would have.

2. Parents were not voiceless. Their voice had nowhere to bind.

The five-district study of reopening decisions found parent input flowed through surveys and virtual town halls used to elicit individual preferences rather than as deliberative forums. A principal quoted in it: "There's not this group that [parents'] voice can be sent through and amplified, and so they're just individuals." (Singer et al.)

The asymmetry was not in volume of input. It was in whether input had anywhere to bind. Unions had a contract; everyone else had a survey. That is a sharper and more fixable complaint than "nobody asked us."

3. Parents were largely deferential at the time. The damage shows up later, in exit and in partisan-asymmetric confidence.

The same study found no evidence of significant parent backlash during 2020-21. One New Orleans parent: "I can't say that they did wrong. They did the right thing". It argues parent deference reinforced district caution. Gallup's series on satisfaction with one's own child's education barely moved (82% → 74%). The PDK Poll shows no decline at all through 2022.

What did move: Gallup confidence in public schools fell from a 2020 rally peak of 41% to 28% in 2022, driven almost entirely by Republicans (34% → 14%) while Democrats held (48% → 43%). The 2022 partisan gap was 29 points against a historical average of 7 (Gallup). And Dee et al. found remote-only instruction caused an additional 1.1-point enrollment decline: a 42% increase in disenrollment, with hybrid showing no effect (NBER w29156).

So: systemic, partisan, lagged, and revealed through behavior. Not contemporaneous parent-reported grievance. A memo claiming the latter is contradicted by the best qualitative evidence.

4. The claim that school closures fed the anti-science turn is unsupported.

Searched directly. Pew's reports on the decline in confidence in scientists (87% in April 2020 → 73% in October 2023) contain no mention of schools, education policy, or closures. The largest recent academic treatment, ~10,000 respondents across 25+ countries: does not examine education policy at all. And confidence in scientists has since recovered to 77% (January 2026) while confidence in schools has not, which runs the wrong way for the story.

The circumstantial case is not nothing: the Republican-specific collapse in school confidence and the Republican-specific deficit in scientist confidence describe the same population. But nobody has connected them. Carry it as a labeled hypothesis or drop it.

5. The COVID trust collapse was more partisan than racial, including at CDC.

This one is uncomfortable because it cuts at the load-bearing assumption. RAND's American Life Panel measured CDC trust May → October 2020 and found the decline was significant among White (−0.8) and Hispanic (−0.7) respondents and not statistically significant among Black respondents (−0.3); by politics, Trump voters −0.8 (significant), Biden voters −0.3 (not) (RAND RR-A308-12).

Historical institutional trauma is real and well documented as a driver of differential response to identical guidance. But the CDC communication failure of 2020-21 did not fall hardest on the communities the equity interventions were designed for. Two distinct mechanisms are being conflated in the first memo, and they call for different fixes.

Section 2The method, and what a link has to carry

A chain runs from a root cause to a quantified outcome. Every link is an explicit assertion that A produced B, and every link has to carry four things:

  1. The claim: stated so it could be false.
  2. Evidence, graded. Strong means causal identification or an authoritative repeated measure. Moderate means correlational, single-survey, or a program evaluation with a comparison group. Weak means anecdote, self-report, or advocacy source. Contested means credible evidence points both ways. Unstudied means I looked and it does not appear to exist.
  3. Counter-evidence, kept as a separate field rather than folded into a hedge. If a link is contested, the memo should show the fight.
  4. Participation instances at that link: what was actually tried, by whom, and whether it demonstrably moved anything: most change, partial change, no change, or never measured.

The last category is doing more work than expected. Across the three cases, 9 of 20 documented participation instances have never had their effect measured at all, including roughly $1.1 billion of federal community-engagement spending.

Chains branch. Several links descend from the same node (a link can declare which earlier link it continues), and two cases have two parallel chains because they have two distinct outcomes participation was asked to improve. The tool enforces continuity: a link's starting node must be its parent's ending node, or the linter rejects it. That constraint is the point: it prevents the chain from quietly becoming a list of related facts.

Section 3Six findings that hold across all three cases

1. In two of three cases, journalism performed a state function

The federal government did not publish county-level COVID case data during the first year of the pandemic. A single New York Times reporter's spreadsheet scaled to 160+ contributors, won a Pulitzer, and was viewed a billion times, then shut down in 2023 because state and local agencies were "reporting their data less frequently and less reliably." In Cape Fear, a StarNews reporter found the contamination by browsing EPA's own UCMR3 database, noticing Wilmington hits, and searching his way to the Knappe paper. The disclosure infrastructure worked. Nobody was reading it.

Both channels performed a notification function no statute assigned to anyone. Both have no funding model and no obligation to exist. This is the most consequential pattern in the project and the first memo did not name it.

2. The only participation instruments that reliably bind are adversarial and legal

Ranked by documented effect on an outcome: teachers' union collective bargaining (moved reopening timing, controlling for case rates); the Cape Fear River Watch citizen suit (produced party status, a $12M penalty, and independent enforcement authority over eleven paragraphs of a consent order); the Consent Order public comment period (produced itemized, agency-acknowledged changes protecting downstream utilities); Tennant/Leach discovery (pierced two decades of concealment).

Deliberative instruments appear exactly once in the entire record (a 30-person sortition panel in Michigan in November 2020), and it was a research exercise, not a decision procedure. I could not find a single US school district that ran a structured deliberative process on reopening. The normative case for deliberation in this project currently rests on theory and on non-US precedent, not on anything these three cases demonstrate.

3. The causal evidence supports access interventions, not trust interventions

This is the finding that runs against the thesis, and it should be confronted rather than managed.

The one trial that cleanly isolated the trusted-messenger mechanism returned zero. The canonical Popular Opinion Leader cluster RCT for COVID vaccine uptake among Black adults began recruiting in February 2024, three years after the question stopped mattering operationally. Meanwhile every instrument that moved uptake bypasses trust entirely.

There is a defensible reading that keeps participation in the argument: what community infrastructure actually delivers is access, a low-barrier site with people who speak your language, no insurance requirement, and no appointment system. Unidos en Salud reached 11,098 people at 70.5% Latinx against a 13.3% citywide share, with 47.3% uninsured and 62.4% with no primary care provider. That is a real and large effect on reach. It is just not evidence about trust, and the memo should stop borrowing the trust framing to describe an access result.

4. Public comment worked once, and we know approximately why

Comment periods are usually theatre. The Chemours Consent Order comment period is the cleanest counter-example in the project: ~380 comments produced a published, itemized agency response adding a new paragraph requiring characterization of PFAS at downstream utility raw water intakes, a sediment assessment, a three-to-fourfold increase in Old Outfall 002 reduction, a 20-year filtration floor, and DEQ's right to split samples.

Note who benefited: downstream communities, the constituency furthest from the facility and least represented in the original negotiation. Four conditions were present that usually are not: a specific draft document rather than an open question; a defined adversary; an intermediary (SELC) running a public session to translate the draft before the deadline; and a live lawsuit in the background. Those are testable conditions, and testing them across other consent orders is a tractable study.

5. Participation instruments are venue-scoped, and win the plant while losing the rule

Cape Fear is the strongest participation record in the project. Nine years on, North Carolina still has no enforceable surface water standard for GenX: the Environmental Management Commission narrowed groundwater standards from the eight compounds DEQ proposed to three, and the 2026 monitoring rules carry no compliance deadline. And on 20 May 2026 EPA proposed rescinding the federal 10 ng/L drinking water limit for GenX (Federal Register; the comment period ran to July 20, 2026).

Meanwhile CFPUA ratepayers have paid $82.8 million for filtration and Chemours has contributed nothing toward it, and CFPUA's own motion to intervene in the consent order was denied as untimely. The citizen group got in; the entity paying the bill did not.

A citizen suit produces relief at one facility. A comment period improves one order. Neither reaches the rule that would protect the next community, because rulemaking is a different venue with different participants on a different clock. Any theory of participation that stops at "the community won" will systematically overstate what these instruments accomplish.

6. Money without measurement

Roughly $1.1 billion in federal participation infrastructure ($323M to 150 community-based organizations, $250M to local governments for health literacy, $500M for community health workers, $29M for vaccine-confidence research) exists in the public record as dollar figures and dose counts and nothing else. I could find no published outcome evaluation of any line item.

Worse: DHS's Inspector General found FEMA had not finalized its equity guidance and lacked systems to collect demographic data on applicants: "without a system of records, FEMA cannot accurately identify and target resources to underserved and marginalized communities." About 19% of federal Community Vaccination Center doses have no demographic data at all. Site-level results ranged from Newark (16.8% of doses to White recipients in an 11%-White city) to Cleveland (73% to White recipients in a 33.8%-White city).

If the memo makes one narrow institutional recommendation, "fund the evaluation at the same time you fund the program" is defensible on this evidence alone.

Case 1COVID-19 school closures

One chain, seven links, terminal outcome: durable systemic confidence loss expressed through exit and adversarial participation. This case has no clean harm-reduction outcome participation could plausibly have improved: the epidemiological tradeoff was genuinely contested. So the chain runs to legitimacy instead.

The uncomfortable thing about S1 and S2

Union collective bargaining is the clearest example in this entire project of participation that demonstrably moved a policy outcome. The complaint cannot be that unions participated. It has to be that they were the only stakeholder whose participation had teeth. That reframes the design question usefully: not "how do we reduce union voice" but "what instrument gives other stakeholders comparable binding capacity", and the honest answer from the record is that nobody built one.

The Annenberg result deserves its own paragraph

A national probability panel tracked actual dose receipt from April 2021 to July 2022 and tested five explanations for the narrowing of the Black–White gap. Only knowledge mediated it, and the authors attribute it to knowledge transmitted from within the Black community (APPC).

Read pessimistically, this rules out both mechanisms the participation programs were designed around. Read constructively, the thing that worked is itself a form of participation: one nobody designed, funded, or measured: information moving through community networks under their own logic. That is a research agenda, not a null result. It also suggests the design question is less "how do we message to communities" and more "what makes a community's own information network fast and accurate."

Two design findings this case hands us directly

A TSCA 5(e) consent order creates an obligation on a company with no scheduled verification and no notification duty to the affected public. Attaching a local monitoring plan with public involvement to every 5(e) order is a narrow, concrete reform this case supports on its own facts, and it is close to the first memo's "FDA-like initial review involving the public" instinct, but scoped to an existing instrument rather than a new institution.

Return-of-results is a real, sustained, eight-year practice here and nobody has evaluated it. Individual blood PFAS results mailed to participants in 2018, an 11-page letter with NASEM clinical guidance and an expert Q&A panel in 2022, a community meeting hosted by the citizen group, further results in 2025. Given how rare sustained return-of-results is, evaluating its effect on trust, retention and mobilization is unusually tractable and would generalize well.

Section 4The "bad company + regulatory failure" typology

The first memo hypothesized that this case type is accumulating and eroding trust in regulators specifically, spilling into unrelated domains like data-center opposition. I went looking. The results are mostly negative, and the negatives are useful.

What is not there

  • The regulator-specific pre/post design essentially does not exist in the US polling record. No before/after measurement of trust in NHTSA around Takata, EPA or CARB around Dieselgate, FDA or DEA around Purdue, CMS or FDA around Theranos, FDA around the 2022 formula shutdown, or FRA and EPA around East Palestine. The 2005 AP-Ipsos release on Vioxx has exactly the right headline (damage localized to the regulator, not the drug class), and no retrievable percentages.
  • Nobody has operationalised concealment-plus-regulatory-inaction as a treatment. The scandal-and-trust literature studies political corruption, not this.
  • The one clean test of cross-level spillover is negative. A Belgian study of 2018 municipal scandals found real effects on trust in local government and mayors, but tested federal and regional trust as a robustness check and found the effect largely confined to the level where the scandal occurred (Acta Politica 58(1)). If the memo asserts spillover, it is asserting something the best available evidence mildly contradicts.
  • The accumulated-tech-distrust explanation for data-center opposition has not been tested against NIMBY, and the descriptive evidence cuts against it. Gallup's March–April 2026 survey finds 71% oppose a local AI data center, but the stated reasons run about 3.5:1 toward material concerns: 50% resource effects (water, energy), 22% quality of life, 20% costs, versus 14% negative views of AI (Gallup). Gallup publishes no breakdown by proximity, so the core NIMBY test is unrun.

What is there, and is worth taking

  • Compensation repairs competence violations and does not repair integrity violations. In two studies, compensation restored trust after competence-based breaches (p<.001) and had no significant effect after integrity-based ones (F=1.85, p=.162), including under overcompensation (PLOS ONE 10(12)). Concealment is definitionally an integrity violation. This is a mechanism that explains the Stanford UK finding the first memo already cites, and it predicts that the Flint $626M and 3M $10.3B settlements did not restore trust. It is lab work; the field replication is the single most tractable gap in this whole typology.
  • Trust damage persists after corruption perceptions recover. Spanish scandals moved ~6.2% of the population from trusting to distrusting local politicians; perceptions reverted to baseline, trust did not (EJPE 55).
  • Boeing 737 MAX is the strongest analogue we do not yet have as a case. Documented concealment ("Boeing withheld crucial information from the FAA… including concealing the very existence of MCAS from 737 MAX pilots"), documented regulatory capture through delegation ("FAA management has undercut the authority and judgment of its own technical experts and sided with Boeing"), 346 deaths, and a complete participation vacuum (House T&I final report; DOT OIG).
  • Memphis xAI is a data-center case that is the bad-company type. A records request found zero permits on file; aerial imagery in April 2025 revealed 35 turbines against a disclosed count roughly half that; thermal imaging confirmed 30+ operating; turbines came out only after an NAACP Clean Air Act notice (SELC). This is citizen monitoring forcing regulatory response, in the domain where the memo currently only has survey data.
  • The monitoring channel is being actively closed. Louisiana's Community Air Monitoring Reliability Act, signed May 2024, bars data from non-EPA-approved technologies (i.e. community monitors) from Clean Air Act enforcement, at the same time EPA has awarded $53M to 133 community monitoring groups (Columbia Climate Law). The funding and the admissibility are moving in opposite directions. That is a sharp, current, previously unmapped finding.

The identification problem this typology has to solve

The largest recent movements in regulator trust are attributed to partisan political attack, not to scandal: FDA confidence fell 73% → 63% and CDC 72% → 64% between September 2024 and August 2025, driven almost entirely by Democrats (Annenberg). Any claim that concealment scandals drive regulator distrust has to net out a much larger partisan signal running through the same period. That is the typology's principal methodological obstacle, not a footnote.

Section 5What this implies for design

Reading the three chains together, the participation instruments that did something have four properties in common, and the ones that did nothing lack them.

PropertyPresent inAbsent from
Binds someone. Produces an obligation, not information. Collective bargaining; consent-order party status with enforcement rights; discovery Advisory councils; surveys; town halls; sortition panels run as research
Arrives before the decision rather than after it. Union negotiation; comment on a draft order Recalls; litigation against a decision already made; disenrollment
Attaches to a specific document the public can mark up. The Chemours draft consent order "Weigh in on reopening"; generic health-communication campaigns
Has an intermediary doing translation. SELC before the comment deadline; librarians and promotoras as information brokers Federal messaging campaigns; comment portals without a guide

Three concrete proposals follow, each scoped to an existing instrument rather than a new institution, which is the form of proposal this evidence can actually support:

  1. A statutory public-notification trigger. When a peer-reviewed detection above a threshold reaches a regulator, notification to the affected water system and public becomes mandatory within a fixed window. This is the instrument whose absence cost Wilmington seven months and whose function was performed, unpaid and unobliged, by a newspaper.
  2. Public-involvement conditions on TSCA 5(e)-type orders. Any consent order permitting discharge under uncertainty carries a local monitoring plan with named community participants and scheduled verification. The Cape Fear order shows this is draftable: ¶29, ¶38 and ¶40 already do a version of it, imposed by a citizen group rather than by statute.
  3. Evaluation funded alongside participation programs. Not a governance reform, but on the evidence here the highest-yield one: $1.1B of participation infrastructure is currently unevaluable, and the strongest arguments for participation are unavailable because nobody measured.

And one thing the evidence does not support: a general claim that deliberative minipublics would have improved these three outcomes. The instrument barely appears in the American record for these cases, and where it appears it changed nothing. The defensible version is a prospective claim: that deliberation on the value tradeoff, held before a coercive instrument is deployed, would change perceived legitimacy, and that claim is testable in the next emergency if it is designed now.

Section 6Research plan

Ranked by how much resolving the question would change the argument, not by how easy it is. Items marked Tier 1 would change what the memo concludes.

Tier 1Closure duration → trust and legitimacy outcomes

The gap: No paper uses remote-learning duration or instructional mode as a treatment with a trust, satisfaction, legitimacy or civic outcome. Singer's 2025 review of the reopening literature has no downstream-consequences section; Jacob (2024) explicitly disclaims the ability to test it. The only mode-as-treatment causal paper measures enrollment.

Why it matters: This is link S4, currently graded Contested, and it is the hinge of the whole schools case. If closure duration predicts a trust outcome, the legitimacy argument is established. If it does not, the case reduces to a story about exit.

Approach: District-level instructional-mode panels (the Dee et al. and DeAngelis-Makridis data exist) merged against geographically resolvable attitude measures: EdChoice/Morning Consult monthly parent tracker has state identifiers; Gallup and PDK have region. State-level is a fallback if district-level attitude data does not exist. Check first whether anyone holds a geocoded parent-trust panel; if not, the fallback is a retrospective survey with recalled district modality, which is weaker but publishable.

Tier 1Does compensation repair trust after an integrity violation? Field test.

The gap: The finding is lab-only (PLOS ONE 2015). No field replication exists, despite four unusually clean natural experiments: Flint's $626M settlement, 3M's $10.3B PFAS water-system settlement (2023), DuPont/Chemours settlements, and VW buybacks.

Why it matters: It is the mechanism that would make the "bad company + regulatory failure" typology cohere, and it predicts something policy-relevant: that settlement-based remedies do not restore the thing that was broken.

Approach: Look for pre/post trust measures around settlement announcement dates in existing panels (Roper archive has post-Flint drinking-water trust polling; check whether any state-level series brackets the 2023 3M settlement). If nothing brackets it, this becomes a commissioned survey, but check the archives first, because the Roper Center holds more of this than is indexed.

Tier 1Excess minority mortality attributable to the spring 2021 vaccination lag

The gap: Both major preventable-death estimates (~234k KFF, ~232k EJE) and the Commonwealth Fund averted-deaths model report by age, not by race. The specific "fair harm reduction" quantity has never been estimated.

Why it matters: It is the terminal node of the fair-harm chain. Without it, the equity argument runs on gap percentages rather than on lives, which is both weaker rhetorically and less useful for cost-benefit.

Approach: The method is a direct extension of the existing ones: apply age-and-race-specific death rates among vaccinated vs unvaccinated to the observed coverage gap trajectory. The jurisdictions reporting vaccination status by death (25–30 states) would need race stratification; check CDC's restricted-use files. Feasible as a collaboration with an author of either existing estimate.

Tier 1Prospective: deliberation before coercion

The gap: Nobody has tested whether a deliberative process convened before a mandate (on the value tradeoff, not the science) changes subsequent compliance or perceived legitimacy.

Why it matters: It is the only way to establish the project's central normative claim causally, and it cannot be done retrospectively because the instrument was never used.

Approach: Design now, deploy at the next opportunity. The nearest available analogue in the meantime is a survey experiment: describe a policy as arrived at by (a) expert determination, (b) stakeholder negotiation, (c) a randomly selected citizen panel, and measure stated compliance and legitimacy. That is cheap, it is a real contribution, and it does not require an emergency.

Tier 2What made the Chemours comment period work?

Comment periods almost never change terms. This one demonstrably did, and DEQ published the itemized changes. The four candidate conditions (specific draft, defined adversary, intermediary translation, live litigation) are testable by comparing against a sample of other state consent orders with published comment responses. This is the most directly actionable design study in the plan.

Tier 2Evaluate the GenX return-of-results program

An eight-year sustained practice with no evaluation. Effects on participant trust, retention, community mobilization and policy. Jane Hoppin's team is the obvious partner; the study has a built-in comparison in the anonymised 2010–2016 archived-sera cohort, whose members received nothing back.

Tier 2Homeschool uptake regressed on remote-learning duration

Dee et al. did this for public enrollment. Nobody has done it for homeschooling, where growth in 2024-25 exceeded pandemic peaks. Data exists (JHU Homeschool Hub for 30 states; district modality panels). Small, clean, and it converts the strongest revealed-preference evidence in the schools case into a dose-response finding.

Tier 2Does a statutory public-notification trigger exist anywhere?

A jurisdiction scan: EU (the 2019 Whistleblower Directive covers environmental protection and requires anonymous external channels), California Prop 65, state right-to-know statutes, and any drinking-water notification rule that fires on research rather than on exceedance of a promulgated standard. If nothing fires on peer-reviewed detection of an unregulated compound, that absence is the finding, and it is the memo's cleanest legislative recommendation.

Tier 2Data centers: distrust vs proximity

Gallup publishes no breakdown by proximity to an existing facility, so the NIMBY test is unrun and the accumulated-distrust hypothesis is untested. A survey experiment varying (a) operator identity, (b) stated local benefit, (c) distance, would settle it. Worth doing only if the memo intends to keep data centers as a case; otherwise Memphis xAI is the better use of the same attention, because it is a documented instance of the concealment type rather than a sentiment finding.

Tier 3Rural resentment: currently unmapped

Cramer's rural consciousness framework is in the repository and appears nowhere in the chains, because it is not clear which link it acts on. It is not the same object as institutional-harm-based distrust: it is a place identity plus a perception of distributive injustice, and it predicts opposition to policies that would materially help the person opposing them. Before adding it, the question to answer is whether resentment-based distrust enters these chains at the trust link at all, or whether it operates on the legitimacy link instead: i.e. whether the objection is "I don't believe you" or "you don't get to decide this." Those imply different instruments, and the Gallup partisan-asymmetry finding in the schools case is the natural place to test it.
