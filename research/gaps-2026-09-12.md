# Gaps and judgment calls after the evidence rebuild, 12 September 2026

The approved revision in `research/proposal-revision-draft.md` is now applied to all three spine files.
Lint passes, the layout check finds no overlaps, and every card type opens the right pane. Nothing is

This file lists what the rebuild could not support, what I decided where the research left a choice,
and what still needs your answer.

## Claims that now stand with no supporting evidence card

These are visible gaps, not oversights. Each one had a card that did not survive the audit.

- **Schools, Proposal 4: "A voice before decisions reduces recalls and lawsuits after them."** Its
  only card cited a hospital study whose figure appears solely in a press release, and it covered
  disclosure after harm. One counter-evidence card remains.
- **GenX, Proposal 3: "A statutory trigger turns a regulator's detection into public notice."** The
  old evidence card was a news profile of one reporter, which shows that no trigger operated. It is
  now a case card. The claim carries counter-evidence and four cases, including California's
  30-day notice law, which is the closest enacted version of the proposal.
- **GenX, Proposal 3: "Once the public knows, agencies issue advisories and open investigations
  within weeks."** Narrowed from "agencies respond within weeks" after a deep-research pass on 12
  September. No study measures how fast agencies act after public disclosure of a contaminant, so
  the claim rests on five dated cases:
  - Supporting the claim: Wilmington (investigation within a week), Flint (county advisory after 5
    days) and Vermont (advisory within weeks).
  - Against it: New Jersey, where the advisory came about 6 months after a reporter published the
    results.
  - Binding steps took months to years: the Flint emergency order, the Hoosick Falls Superfund
    listing and the Chemours consent order. The case cards say so.

  The two evidence cards are strong studies of mailed water quality reports, but they measure
  utility violations, not agency speed. They support the idea that disclosure changes behavior, not
  the timing in the claim itself.

## Claims where a real search found nothing direct

- **A mandate with public backing meets less backlash** (vaccines, Proposal 2). Nothing compares the
  same mandate with public backing against that mandate imposed. The one study that runs that
  comparison on a coercive pandemic rule finds no gain, and it sits on the card as counter-evidence.
  The supporting cards carry the two halves of the mechanism separately: mandates generate opposition
  among people who do not back them, and publicly decided policies draw more acceptance from people
  who lost the argument.
- **Delivery through trusted local institutions raises trust in the vaccine** (vaccines, Proposal 3).
  No study assigned delivery through a church, barbershop, or community health worker program and
  then measured trust in the vaccine. The best direct card compares people who chose a faith-based
  site with people who chose a clinic.
- **Higher vaccination in hard-hit neighborhoods narrows racial gaps in deaths** (vaccines, Proposal
  3). No study estimates that. The cards give a strong area-based estimate of deaths averted, a
  projection about who geographic targeting reaches, and mediation by income rather than race.
- **Equitable access narrows the racial gap in vaccination** (vaccines, Proposal 3). After the audit,
  support is one authoritative record that the national gap closed. Nothing links an access program
  to a narrower racial gap.
- **When residents help set the measures, evaluations get done and published** (vaccines, Proposal 5).
  No comparative or causal evidence exists. The claim carries one weak card and the Malawi scorecard
  case.
- **Taking part in evaluation raises trust in government** (vaccines, Proposal 5). Nothing tests
  taking part in evaluating a public program against trust in government. The cards measure taking
  part in deciding, not evaluating, and say so.
- **Standing local capacity turns facility wins into statewide standards** (GenX, Proposal 5). No
  peer-reviewed evidence that funded local capacity produces state rule adoption. The closest
  verified finding, that organized or grant-funded communities got more health-protective cleanups,
  reaches remedy choices at federal sites, and now sits under claim 1 where it belongs.
- **No counter-evidence found** for "Residents who decide how money is spent trust how it is used"
  (vaccines, Proposal 4) or for "Residents who track public spending know more about where the money
  went" (vaccines, Proposal 5).

## Claim wording the evidence does not reach

- **GenX, Proposal 4, "Community-appointed panels produce findings that carry weight."** The C8
  Science Panel was chosen by DuPont and the lawyers for the class, not by residents. Both cards now
  say so, and the evidence card carries that limit as a caveat. The claim needs either a rewording or
  an acknowledged gap.
- **GenX, Proposal 5, "Adopted standards protect the next community before exposure."** The two
  replacement studies measure exposure falling after a standard took effect. Neither shows a
  community protected before exposure began.

## Decisions I made where the research offered a choice

- **Card counts.** Several claims came back with eleven or more candidate cards. I selected the
  distinct and better-identified ones and left the rest out rather than stacking near-duplicates. The
  named omissions: Arnesen and Peters 2018 and Schmelz and Bowles 2022 (vaccines P2 claim 3
  evidence), Arnesen 2017 and Sprengholz et al. 2023 (same claim, counter-evidence), the Swiss
  COVID-19 law vote case (weak and only partly verified), Carr 2018, Kim 2020, Sin 2021 and Mao 2021
  (vaccines P3 claim 6), Casey et al. 2012 (covered by the King and Samii synthesis), Olken 2007 and
  Charnley and Engelbert 2005 (off the claim as worded), and Castell et al. 2017 (air sensors, in a
  drinking water proposal).
- **Goovaerts et al. 2025** tests exactly what the vaccines Proposal 2 description promises, that a
  published reasoned response repairs most of the legitimacy lost when politicians override an
  assembly. The draft does not list it, so I left it out. It is ready to add under claim 1 if you
  want it.
- **Cards used in one place only.** Where the same study fit two proposals, I kept it in one. The
  Flint resident sampling cards sit under GenX Proposal 2; GenX Proposal 3 uses the distinct set
  (Goovaerts, Kosiarski, Weed, Vijayashanthar). Bennear and Olmstead stays under Proposal 2's
  disclosure claim rather than being repeated in Proposals 3 and 5.
- **Mechanism labels.** Schools Proposal 4 reads "Yes or no community vote" rather than the draft's
  "Y/N Community Vote", since the house style spells things out. GenX labels follow question 2 of the
  draft.
- **Barr, Mugisha, Serneels and Zeitlin 2012** is a working paper with no published version found. It
  is the only study that tests residents defining the measures themselves, so it is carded under
  vaccines Proposal 5 claim 2 with a caveat saying it is unpublished and should be checked.

## Figures that could not be verified, and what happened to them

- Flint "more than $600 million in relief": cut. Michigan reports more than $350 million from the
  state plus $100 million federal. The $600 million figure is a separate 2020 settlement with
  victims.
- Tonawanda "14 of 19 charges": corrected to eleven Clean Air Act counts and three Resource
  Conservation and Recovery Act counts. No source gives a denominator of 19.
- "$500 million for community health workers" in the vaccines proposals: cut. That program was $332
  million and came from the CARES Act. The award dates also moved from March-April 2021 to June-July
  2021, which is when the awards were actually made.
- "Federal money went out with no say for the people it was for": cut. No oversight or peer-reviewed
  source documents such complaints. Replaced with what the reviews do support, that the money was
  slow to reach communities, held up by long contracting and thin local capacity.
- Print years left off three labels because only an online-first date could be confirmed: Johnson,
  Carlson and Reynolds in Political Behavior, and Shybalkina and Bifulco in Public Budgeting and
  Finance. Wang et al. is labeled 2026 because that is the year the index gives.
- The Grossmann et al. 2021 quote flagged in the earlier handoff is still not used anywhere.
