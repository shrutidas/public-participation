# Measured Outcomes, Proposed Public Participation, Implications, and Claims

## COVID-19 School Closures and Reopenings

### Measured Outcomes

#### Measured Outcome 1: Public school enrollment fell

**Headline:** Public school enrollment fell by 1.4 million students between fall 2019 and fall 2020 and has not recovered. Remote-only instruction caused a measurable share of the exit.

**What it measures:** National public school enrollment fell from 50.8 million in fall 2019 to 49.4 million in fall 2020 and stayed near that level. Remote-only instruction caused 1.1 percentage points of additional disenrollment, concentrated in kindergarten and elementary grades.

#### Measured Outcome 2: Chronic absenteeism stayed high

**Headline:** Chronic absenteeism stayed high long after schools reopened. Roughly 22% of students nationally missed a tenth or more of school days through 2024-25.

**What it measures:** Chronic absenteeism means missing 10 percent or more of school days. It stayed high through 2024-25, at roughly 22% of students nationally. In about half of surveyed urban districts, more than 30% of students were chronically absent. Kindergartners who were never in school during closures show rates 48% above the 2019 baseline.

#### Measured Outcome 3: Confidence in public schools fell

**Headline:** Confidence in public schools fell to 28% by 2022. The decline came almost entirely from Republicans, while parents kept rating their own schools far better than the institution.

**What it measures:** Gallup’s measure of confidence in public schools fell from its 2020 rally to 28% by 2022. Republican confidence fell from 34% to 14% while Democratic confidence held. The partisan gap reached 29 points, against a 7-point historical average.

#### Measured Outcome 4: National test scores fell

**Headline:** National test scores fell sharply after the COVID-19 pandemic, but recent studies show the decline began years earlier.

**What it measures:** National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) long-term trend age-9 scores fell 5 points in reading and 7 in mathematics from 2020 to 2022, the first mathematics decline in the series. Districts with longer remote instruction show larger declines.

#### Measured Outcome 5: Studies disagree on whether closing schools cut COVID-19 infections and deaths

**Headline:** Studies disagree on how much closing schools cut COVID-19 infections and deaths. Open schools raised infections among teachers, and the effect on the wider community ranged from large to none.

**What it measures:** When US states closed schools in spring 2020, weekly COVID-19 deaths fell 58 percent, but states issued other orders at the same time. In Sweden, teachers at schools that stayed open caught COVID-19 at twice the rate of teachers who moved online, and parents' rates rose a little. In the US, people living with a child in in-person school had higher COVID-19 risk, and the gap disappeared where schools used seven or more safety measures. In fall 2020, US counties that reopened in person saw no change in cases in most regions, but a rise of 9.8 to 21.3 cases per 100,000 people per week in the South. In Japan, matched towns showed no drop in cases from closing schools.

### Proposal 1: A randomly selected group of residents deliberates on reopening

**Mechanism:** Citizens' Assembly

**Proposed implication:** A reopening decision that fits local conditions and priorities, made by people who had a say, is seen as legitimate and holds confidence in public schools.

**Measured outcome it names:** Confidence in public schools fell

**Claims:**

1. An assembly can be convened in time to inform the decision.
2. A randomly selected group weighs local conditions and priorities when it sets a trade-off.
3. People see a decision as more legitimate when people like them had a say in it.
4. People who see a decision as legitimate keep confidence in the institution that made it.

### Proposal 2: A council of parents, teachers, and experts designs the reopening plan

**Mechanism:** Stakeholder Advisory Council

**Proposed implication:** People who know the buildings design reopening plans that fit each school, which builds trust between parents and teachers, keeps teachers, and cuts chronic absenteeism.

**Measured outcome it names:** Chronic absenteeism stayed high

**Claims:**

1. People who know the building put options on the table that an open-or-closed debate misses.
2. Ventilation and outdoor instruction make in-person school safer.
3. Plans designed close to the school fit its local constraints.
4. Teachers who have a say in school decisions are more likely to stay.
5. Parents and teachers who plan together build stronger relationships.
6. Schools that work closely with families see fewer chronic absences.

### Proposal 3: Publish local case, hospital, and school data early

**Mechanism:** Proactive Data Disclosure

**Proposed implication:** Shared local data holds confidence in public schools.

**Measured outcome it names:** Confidence in public schools fell

**Claims:**

1. Local data can be published from the first weeks, and no one else fills the gap.
2. Publishing uncertainty with the data does not cost trust.
3. Shared local data narrows the gap in how groups see the risk.
4. Shared understanding holds confidence in the institutions that decide.

### Proposal 4: A parent and community body votes yes or no before any closure

**Mechanism:** Binding Community Referendum

**Proposed implication:** Having to win approval forces a public case for every closure, which makes the decision accountable and holds confidence in public schools.

**Measured outcome it names:** Confidence in public schools fell

**Claims:**

1. A standing parent and community body can govern schools competently.
2. Having to justify a decision to an outside body puts the evidence on the public record.
3. A body that can say no holds decision makers to account.
4. A binding role before decisions raises trust and engagement.
5. A voice before decisions reduces recalls and lawsuits after them.

## COVID-19 Vaccine Mandates

### Measured Outcomes

#### Measured Outcome 1: Vaccines prevented millions of deaths and hospitalizations

**Headline:** Vaccines prevented more than 3 million deaths and 18 million hospitalizations in the US in the program's first two years.

**What it measures:** The vaccination program prevented more than 18 million additional hospitalizations and over 3 million additional deaths in the US in its first two years.

#### Measured Outcome 2: Vaccination could have saved about 232,000 more adults

**Headline:** Two independent estimates find that vaccines could have saved roughly 232,000 adults who died between June 2021 and March 2022.

**What it measures:** Two independent estimates find that vaccines could have saved roughly 232,000 to 234,000 adults who died between June 2021 and March 2022.

#### Measured Outcome 3: The racial gap in vaccination narrowed

**Headline:** The Black-White vaccination gap shrank from about 14 points at the April 2021 peak to 5 points by mid-2022. No one has estimated the mortality cost of the spring 2021 lag by race.

**What it measures:** The Black-White vaccination gap fell from about 14 points at the April 2021 peak to 5 points by July 2022. By the end of November 2021, at-least-one-dose coverage was similar across groups. No one has estimated the mortality cost of the spring 2021 lag by race.

#### Measured Outcome 4: Mandates raised vaccination abroad but not in US states

**Headline:** First doses jumped 66% after countries abroad announced mandates. US state mandates did not change uptake, and booster and flu vaccination fell afterward.

**What it measures:** Weekly first doses rose 66% after Canada, France, Italy, and Germany announced mandates, with cumulative gains of 5 to 12 points. US state mandates did not change uptake, and booster and flu vaccination fell afterward. The Supreme Court stayed the federal rule before anyone enforced it, so the US evidence comes from state mandates.

### Proposal 1: Publish vaccine safety and case data early, with regular public briefings

**Mechanism:** Proactive Data Disclosure

**Proposed implication:** Regular public data and briefings build trust in government, raise compliance with health rules, and reduce fear and misinformation.

**Measured outcome it names:** None

**Claims:**

1. Openness about bad news keeps trust in public health authorities.
2. People who trust their government follow its health rules more closely.
3. Clear, regular official information reduces fear.
4. Timely official information limits the spread of misinformation.
5. A public data function at national scale is feasible.

### Proposal 2: Citizens' assembly shapes any vaccine mandate before it is imposed

**Mechanism:** Citizens' Assembly

**Proposed implication:** A mandate residents helped shape holds trust in health guidance.

**Measured outcome it names:** None

**Claims:**

1. Deliberation before a decision raises its perceived legitimacy, most among people who distrust government.
2. People comply with rules they see as legitimate.
3. A mandate with public backing meets less backlash.

### Proposal 3: Neighborhood task forces decide where and how vaccines are delivered

**Mechanism:** Neighborhood Co-Governance Board

**Proposed implication:** Community-run delivery raises trust and uptake, narrows the racial vaccination gap, lowers deaths, and strengthens neighborhood institutions.

**Measured outcome it names:** The racial gap in vaccination narrowed

**Claims:**

1. Community task forces put access where the barriers are.
2. Easier access raises uptake more than persuasion does.
3. Delivery through trusted local institutions raises trust in the vaccine.
4. Equitable access narrows the racial gap in vaccination.
5. Higher vaccination in hard-hit neighborhoods prevents deaths and narrows racial gaps in deaths.
6. Organizing delivery together strengthens community ties and reduces isolation.

### Proposal 4: Residents allocate community support funds through participatory budgeting

**Mechanism:** Participatory Budgeting

**Proposed implication:** Residents who allocate the funds get spending that fits their needs and trust how it is used.

**Measured outcome it names:** None

**Claims:**

1. Residents who allocate public money direct it to what their neighborhoods lack.
2. Residents who decide how money is spent trust how it is used.
3. Taking part in budgeting draws residents into civic life.

### Proposal 5: Residents define the measures of success for outreach funds

**Mechanism:** Participatory Monitoring and Evaluation

**Proposed implication:** Residents who define success see where the money went, which brings more systematic evaluation, more engagement, more trust in government, and a stronger sense that they can influence it.

**Measured outcome it names:** None

**Claims:**

1. When residents help set the measures, evaluations get done and published.
2. Community monitoring of a public service improves the service.
3. Residents who track public spending know more about where the money went.
4. Taking part in evaluation raises trust in government and people's sense that they can influence it.

## GenX Contamination in the Cape Fear River

### Measured Outcomes

#### Measured Outcome 1: Residents drank water contaminated with GenX

**Headline:** Roughly 200,000 water customers, and up to 300,000 people downstream, drank water carrying GenX at a mean of 631 ng/L. The discharge ran from 1980 to 2017.

**What it measures:** Roughly 200,000 Cape Fear Public Utility Authority (CFPUA) customers, and 250,000 to 300,000 people downstream, drank water with mean GenX concentrations of 631 ng/L. Levels at the intake reached about 4,500 ng/L. The discharge ran from 1980 to 2017.

#### Measured Outcome 2: Residents carried high PFAS levels in their blood

**Headline:** Fluoroethers made up about a quarter of the summed PFAS in the blood of tested Wilmington residents. Median levels fell 34 to 65 percent within six months of discharge controls.

**What it measures:** Fluoroethers made up about a quarter of the summed PFAS in the blood of 344 Wilmington residents. Four legacy PFAS sat above national levels in 97 percent or more of participants. Median fluoroether levels fell 34 to 65 percent within six months of discharge controls. Tests did not detect GenX above 2 ng/mL; the measured load is fluoroethers and legacy PFAS. Blood levels matter because studies tie PFAS exposure to disease, which the next card covers.

#### Measured Outcome 3: Studies tie PFAS exposure to higher disease risk

**Headline:** The PFAS in residents' blood raise their disease risk. Wilmington residents with more PFAS had higher cholesterol. Large PFOA studies elsewhere show kidney and testicular cancer, and more deaths from kidney disease among exposed workers.

**What it measures:** In the Wilmington cohort, residents with more PFOS and PFNA in their blood had higher total and non-HDL cholesterol, about 5 to 6 mg/dL per quartile, with the strongest link in older adults. Twelve percent of tested residents exceeded the blood level at which the National Academies recommend medical screening. PFOA was elevated in 97 percent or more of tested residents. For PFOA, the C8 Science Panel found probable links to kidney cancer, testicular cancer, thyroid disease, ulcerative colitis, high cholesterol, and pregnancy-induced hypertension. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified PFOA carcinogenic to humans in 2023. Among 5,791 DuPont workers, deaths from kidney cancer and other kidney disease rose with PFOA exposure. In animal studies, GenX produces liver and pancreatic tumors. No human disease or mortality study of GenX exists.

#### Measured Outcome 4: Water customers paid for filtration

**Headline:** Water customers paid for the cleanup. Carbon filtration in Wilmington cost $82.8 million, which an 8 percent rate increase paid for, and reverse osmosis in Brunswick County cost $158.7 million.

**What it measures:** The Cape Fear Public Utility Authority (CFPUA) built granular activated carbon filtration for $43 million, or $82.8 million with maintenance, and paid for it with an 8 percent customer rate increase. Brunswick County built reverse osmosis for $158.7 million.

#### Measured Outcome 5: The consent order cut air emissions 99 percent

**Headline:** The 2019 Consent Order cut air emissions 99 percent by the end of 2019, imposed a $12 million penalty, and delivered alternative drinking water to affected well owners.

**What it measures:** The 2019 Consent Order cut air emissions 99 percent by the end of 2019 through a thermal oxidizer operating at 99.99 percent efficiency. It imposed a $12 million penalty and delivered alternative drinking water to affected well owners. Fluoroether levels in residents' blood fell 34 to 65 percent within six months of discharge controls.

#### Measured Outcome 6: North Carolina set no enforceable PFAS standard

**Headline:** Nine years of the case's most extensive participation got one plant cleaned up but no enforceable statewide standard. In May 2026 the federal government proposed rescinding the GenX limit.

**What it measures:** Nine years of the case's most extensive participation produced remediation at one plant and no enforceable North Carolina surface water standard. The state commission set aside surface water standards in favor of facility-authored minimization plans with no compliance deadline. In May 2026 the federal government proposed rescinding the GenX drinking water limit.

### Proposal 1: Accessible channels for workers to report chemical hazards, at local, state, and national level

**Mechanism:** Protected Worker Disclosure

**Proposed implication:** Workers who can report safely bring hazards to light before exposure spreads.

**Measured outcome it names:** None

**Claims:**

1. The reporting duties and whistleblower channels that exist today leave hazards unreported.
2. Workers see the harm first, but few report without strong protection.
3. Channels outside the chain of command draw reports that formal channels miss.
4. Worker disclosure reaches the regulator and triggers scrutiny years before litigation does.

### Proposal 2: A body of affected residents convenes whenever a chemical is approved for manufacture or discharge

**Mechanism:** Community Advisory Board

**Proposed implication:** Residents who review chemical approvals cut exposure through drinking water.

**Measured outcome it names:** Residents drank water contaminated with GenX

**Claims:**

1. Residents who can report and sample find contamination before officials do.
2. Notice at approval gives the public knowledge from day one, and people act on what they know.
3. Disclosure pressure cuts discharges before enforcement arrives.
4. Public seats in the review produce monitoring commitments and get them verified.
5. Earlier detection and verified compliance shorten the exposure window.

### Proposal 3: Residents are told of any detection in drinking water and direct the follow-up testing

**Mechanism:** Citizen Science

**Proposed implication:** Residents told of a detection, and given a role in the response, cut years of exposure through drinking water.

**Measured outcome it names:** Residents drank water contaminated with GenX

**Claims:**

1. A statutory trigger turns a regulator's detection into public notice.
2. Once the public knows, agencies issue advisories and open investigations within weeks.
3. Residents who direct testing find exposure that official sampling misses.
4. Earlier response avoids years of exposure.

### Proposal 4: Affected residents appoint independent experts to verify health data

**Mechanism:** Independent Science Panel

**Proposed implication:** Residents who chose the reviewers trust the risk information.

**Measured outcome it names:** None

**Claims:**

1. Community-appointed panels produce findings that carry weight.
2. Independent verification builds a trusted channel for risk information.
3. A trusted channel sustains study participation and protective behavior.

### Proposal 5: Federal grants fund standing local monitoring and legal capacity

**Mechanism:** Funded Community Oversight

**Proposed implication:** Standing local capacity carries the fight to statewide rule adoption.

**Measured outcome it names:** North Carolina set no enforceable PFAS standard

**Claims:**

1. Federal funding builds standing local capacity.
2. Standing local capacity turns facility wins into statewide standards.
3. Adopted standards protect the next community before exposure.
